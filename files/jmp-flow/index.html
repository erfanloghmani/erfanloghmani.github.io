Large language models in marketing

Erfan Loghmani 4 / 26
Today LLMs are everywhere — and they are becoming more and more used in
marketing as well. What are large language models? They are large models
trained on a massive corpus of text: books, articles, webpages. That
training has made them good at language and conversation. For marketing,
you can ask them to generate headlines, write emails, or create product
photos and videos. We will zoom in on news headlines as the running example.

Erfan Loghmani 5 / 26
Are LLM outputs optimized for what a business actually cares about — clicks,
conversions, engagement? The short answer is no: pretrained models are trained
for language and conversation, not firm objectives. But a firm can specialize
a model using the historical observational data it already collects — for
example, article bodies, the headlines that were served, and their click-through
rates, at very large scale.

Sources of data for alignment

Erfan Loghmani 6 / 26
A firm that wants to align a language model has two sources of outcome data.
On the experimental side, A/B tests of headlines on the same story give an
unbiased comparison — here, two headlines sharing one image. On the
observational side, the firm sees the packages that actually ran over time:
one headline per story, with a date and a click-through rate. Those logs are
abundant, but they can embed spurious correlations. Experiments are cleaner,
yet costly and noisy in small samples.

Research questions

Erfan Loghmani 7 / 26
Three research questions structure the talk. First: is aligning on historical
observational data safe? The Monday-style concern is that correlational patterns
— e.g. political stories getting higher engagement — can be internalized by the
model. Second: can experimental and observational sources be combined to balance
bias and variance? The paper's answer is bias-corrected inverse-variance
weighting. Third: under a limited experimentation budget, which content should
be tested? We propose gating on where ridge and logistic predictions disagree.

Background: Generative model alignment

What each stage uses

AppendixDirect alignment (DPO)
Erfan Loghmani 9 / 26
Alignment specializes a pretrained model toward preferred outcomes. The classic
RL-based pipeline has three stages: supervised fine-tuning on prompt–output
pairs, a reward model that maps prompt and output to a score (e.g. CTR), then
reinforcement learning that pushes the model to generate higher-scoring outputs.
Direct methods such as DPO skip the separate reward and RL loop; details are in
the appendix. In either case, the model is only as good as the outcome signal
you feed it — which is the hinge for the rest of the talk.

Erfan Loghmani 10 / 26
Three literatures frame the paper. Marketing shows unstructured content matters and
generative models can produce it at scale, but pretrained models are not tuned to
business objectives — experiments are the cleanest fix for alignment. Standard
alignment inherits the quality of the outcome signal; when observational, models
latch onto artifacts. Causal reward methods need confounders identified. This work
treats observational data as biased but useful and asks how experiments should be
valued and combined. Methodologically we sit in double-ML and causal-text, but the
action is high-dimensional content — extending scalar combination logic to textual
actions inside a generative pipeline, and asking which content to experiment on.

AppendixTemporal patterns
Erfan Loghmani 15 / 26
The Upworthy archive records real randomized tests of headlines and images before
publication. Because traffic is randomized within each test, one observes experimental
outcomes — and can reconstruct what a firm would have seen with only one variant
per story. Headline choice has large economic weight: committing all traffic to the
best, second-best, or worst arm yields +27%, +9%, or −26% clicks relative to running
every test. The archive also has rich structure — temporal CTR swings, topic and
story heterogeneity — so performance cannot be read off without conditioning on
when and what was tested.

Benchmarking single-source methods

Three options for reward modeling

Question
Which performs best? How important is the data versus the method?
Erfan Loghmani 17 / 26
Before combining sources, benchmark what each source can do alone. With only
observational data — one headline per story, plus CTR — embed abstract and
headline and fit a CTR-prediction model. With experimental data, one can either
fit the same CTR-prediction loss on all variants, or switch to a ranking
objective such as logistic regression on within-test pairs. All three pipelines
are scored the same way: given abstract and candidate headlines (no CTRs),
predict which ranks higher, and evaluate ROC-AUC against held-out experimental
CTRs on pairs with distinguishable click rates. That sets up the question:
which pipeline wins, and is it the data source or the modeling choice that
matters more?

Method > Data

  • Experimental data + ranking loss is best
    • ~+0.067 ROC-AUC over CTR prediction on the same experimental data
  • Experimental vs observational CTR prediction: almost no gain
    • Full experimental variation adds only ~+0.005 on average

Two sources of data for alignment

Erfan Loghmani 20 / 26
Once both sources are available, can their combination beat using either alone?
Experimental data is unbiased but noisy and costly; observational data is abundant
but biased. Pooling two CTR models is straightforward because they share a target.
The interesting case pairs logistic ranking on experiments — the best use of that
data — with CTR prediction on observational data. Those losses live on different
domains, so parameter-level merges fail.

Proposed method — Challenges

Erfan Loghmani 21 / 26
Three challenges for combining the sources: outputs live in different domains,
weights must adapt to sample size, and observational bias must be estimated
honestly. Map both branches to P(h1 beats h2), then combine with a weight that
depends on variances and bias; estimate that bias by splitting the experimental
set. The schematic appears after the challenges.

Proposed method — Inverse-variance weighting

  • Choose $w_O$ to minimize MSE
    • $$\mathrm{MSE} = w_O^2\mathrm{Bias}(\hat\theta_O)^2 + w_O^2\mathrm{Var}(\hat\theta_O) + (1-w_O)^2\mathrm{Var}(\hat\theta_E)$$
  • Optimal weight
    • $$w_O^\star = \frac{\mathrm{Var}(\hat\theta_E)}{\mathrm{Bias}(\hat\theta_O)^2 + \mathrm{Var}(\hat\theta_O) + \mathrm{Var}(\hat\theta_E)}$$
  • Behavior matches the expected behavior
    • Few experiments → large $\mathrm{Var}(\hat\theta_E)$ → $w_O^\star \to 1$ (lean observational)
    • Large observational bias → $w_O^\star \to 0$ (lean experimental)
AppendixHonest bias estimationAppendixHausman test (select vs combine)
Erfan Loghmani 22 / 26
Once both branches estimate the same win probability, form a convex combination
and pick the weight that minimizes MSE. The closed form is a bias-corrected
inverse-variance weight: noisy experiments borrow strength from observational
data; large observational bias defers to the experiment.

Firms can run experiments for a period — a short or long window — but typically

Conclusion

  • Substantive findings
    • Observational alignment alone can be problematic because fine-tuned model might internalize confounders
    • Experimental data help mainly when the model uses a comparative objective, rather than a performance prediction objective
  • Methodological development
    • Bias-corrected IVW for optimally combining experimental and observational data
    • Ridge–logistic disagreement for selecting informative tests to experiment on
  • Managerial implications
    • Don't use observational data alone: experimentation is needed
    • You can combine even a small experimental sample with the abundant observational data effectively with the proposed IVW method
    • Strategies to choose which tests to experiment on. The best strategy depends on experimentation time horizon
Erfan Loghmani 26 / 26
This paper asked how a firm should use its data to optimize unstructured marketing
content generation by AI. This paper's answer has three parts. First, optimizing a generative
model on historical outcomes alone can backfire, teaching it to reproduce confounders
rather than quality. Second, experimental data are valuable, but mostly when paired
with a model that targets the ranking objective, not performance prediction; the
modeling choice matters more than the experimental variation itself. Third, when a
firm holds both experimental and observational data, the two are best combined rather
than used in isolation. The bias-corrected, inverse-variance estimator does exactly
this, leaning on abundant observational data when experiments are scarce and on the
experiment as it accumulates, and it outperforms either source alone, surpassing full
observational performance with experiments on just over 1% of decisions. Finally,
when experimental budgets are strictly limited, strategic test selection becomes
critical. This paper finds that the optimal allocation of this budget depends heavily on the
time horizon; specifically, over longer collection windows, prioritizing tests where
historical and experimental predictions most disagree emerges as the most effective
strategy.

These results carry a practical lesson for firms weighing whether and how to
experiment. Firms often have an abundant set of historical observational data, while
experimentation is costly. In this paper's time-based simulation, no amount of pure
experimentation beats a model trained on historical data once the opportunity cost of
testing is counted. Only by combining the two sources does a small amount of
experimentation yield a significant net gain.

This study has limitations that point to directions for future work. Validating methods
that combine experimental and observational data requires settings where the full
experimental variation is observed, since that is what reveals whether the correlations
in observational data are genuine or spurious. Such data is scarce, and it was possible
to test this approach only on the Upworthy archive, which varies textual headlines over
news articles. More data sources are needed to assess how the approach generalizes,
particularly to settings with richer unstructured variation such as images, video, or
multimodal creative elements. Additionally, while the inverse-variance weighting
provides a robust and tractable combination mechanism without structural assumptions,
future research could explore how task-specific structure might yield fusion methods
that more effectively mitigate confounding and combine data sources.

Appendix — Honest bias estimationAppendix

Bias-corrected inverse-variance combination (IVW)

BackProposed method — Inverse-variance weighting
Erfan Loghmani xvi / xxx
The IVW estimator first fits an observational CTR model and an experimental
logistic. Bias of the observational branch is estimated honestly by cross-fitting
on experimental pairs: train the logistic on one half, score the held-out half
with both models, and average the gap. For each evaluation pair, map both
branches to a win probability, then combine with the bias-corrected
inverse-variance weight.

Appendix — Hausman testAppendix

Classic approach: select IV or OLS — not combine them

BackProposed method — Inverse-variance weighting
Erfan Loghmani xvii / xxx
A Hausman test compares an IV estimate to OLS. If they differ, OLS is biased and
you use IV; if they agree, prefer OLS for its lower variance. That rule selects
one estimator. The paper instead asks whether the two sources can be combined —
keeping observational precision when bias is small and leaning on experiments
when bias is large — via bias-corrected inverse-variance weights.
